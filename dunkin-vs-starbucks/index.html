Dunkin' Peaks at 9 A.M. Starbucks Peaks at Noon. What Does That Say About Their Customers?

The coffee rivals draw overlapping audiences. Their daily routines, and the way customers pay, tell different stories.

Data analysis · September 2026

By the time Starbucks reaches its busiest hour, Dunkin's morning peak is three hours behind it. Dunkin' traffic peaks at 9 a.m., according to phone-location estimates covering the year through July 2026. Starbucks peaks at noon.

Among 1,720 chains with at least 100 locations in the data, Dunkin' ranks in the top 4% for how much of its traffic falls in the early morning: 31.8% of its visits come between 5 and 10 a.m. Starbucks, at 19.8%, sits almost exactly at the median chain. That difference offers a useful starting point. Both chains sell a familiar daily purchase. But do they occupy the same place in customers' routines?

Starbucks stretches further into the day, and it gets a slightly larger share of its visits on Saturday: 17.0%, compared with Dunkin's 15.9%. The difference is modest, but it holds in all 42 states with enough stores from both chains to compare.

Starbucks also draws substantially more traffic. Its average location receives an estimated 3,360 visits a week, against 1,600 for Dunkin'. Comparing stores within the same state narrows the gap somewhat, but Starbucks remains busier in 41 of the 42 states studied.

Dunkin', meanwhile, shows a stronger pattern of repeat purchasing in store-level card data. A Dunkin' customer made 2.21 recorded card purchases a month at the same store, against 1.67 at Starbucks. Its average card transaction was smaller, $9.22 against $12.47, leaving recorded monthly spending per customer almost equal at about $20. An average transaction is not the price of a coffee, and a card purchase is not a complete count of café visits.

Dunkin' led on that purchase frequency in 38 of 42 states, and its average transaction was lower in 41. The pattern fits the daily schedules: more frequent, smaller purchases at the chain whose traffic is concentrated earlier.

Those card transactions need careful reading, particularly at Starbucks. Over the latest year, cardholders paid $748 million into Starbucks cards and apps, more than the $682 million recorded as direct café purchases. Loading an app is a payment to Starbucks, not a café visit, and later purchases made with that balance may not appear as new charges on a bank card.

That matters for claims about frequency and spending. Direct café card purchases alone put annual spending at roughly $115 per shopper at both chains. That comparison leaves out a major Starbucks payment channel, so it cannot establish that the typical customer spends the same total at each.

It also changes the apparent competitive trend. Looking only at direct café payments makes Dunkin' appear to gain ground, from 0.56 of Starbucks' spending in 2022 to 0.60 in 2026. Including card and app reloads produces a steadier relationship: Dunkin's tracked spending has stayed at about 28% of Starbucks' every year since 2021.

The customer divide is less dramatic than the difference in schedules. Households earning more than $150,000 account for 33% of Starbucks' tracked spending and 29% of Dunkin's, and Starbucks gets a slightly larger share from people under 35. Those are spending shares, not counts of customers, and they describe a modest tilt: the two profiles move together across nearly every group.

Geography contributes to the contrast. About 53% of Dunkin' locations in the data are in the Northeast, compared with 13% of Starbucks locations. Yet the differences in purchase frequency and transaction size persist when the chains are compared within the same states.

Customers also cross between them. In July 2025, 32% of Dunkin's card customers also made a Starbucks purchase that month, and 23% of Starbucks' bought at Dunkin'. Because the underlying cross-shopping lists are truncated, those figures are minimum overlaps, not a complete accounting of shared customers.

The data leave room for both brands in the same person's week. What distinguishes them most clearly is the rhythm of the business: Dunkin's morning concentration, Starbucks' broader day, and two payment systems that make a simple count of card transactions an incomplete picture of the coffee habit.
