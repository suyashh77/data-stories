Groceries Left the City, and Didn't Come Back

City grocery stores in North Carolina lost about a sixth of their footing between 2019 and 2021, while rural stores gained a third. Phone data and card data agree, and five years on the cities have not won it back.

When the pandemic hit, people did not stop buying groceries. They changed where they bought them. We tracked about 2,200 North Carolina grocery stores from 2017 to 2026 and split them by how built-up their neighbourhood is.

First, how we compare stores fairly

Phone panels grow and shrink, and every store gets busier in December. So we never compare raw visit counts. We compare each store with the average North Carolina store of the same kind in the same week.

City stores lost their share of the state's grocery trips

In 2019, stores in city neighbourhoods drew 17.7% of the visits to the grocery stores we track. By 2021 it was 15.0%, and it has stayed there. Card spending tells the same story from a separate dataset.

Caveat: the city share was already slipping before 2020, by about a point a year. COVID sped it up: against similar stores, city stores lost ground nearly three times as fast in 2019–21 as in 2017–19.

All three kinds of place, side by side

Indexed to 2019, the gap opens within months. By 2022, city stores sat 17% below their 2019 level against similar stores, and rural stores 20% above.

The denser the neighbourhood, the bigger the drop

This is not just three buckets. Sort all 2,262 stores by how dense their neighbourhood is and the change slides smoothly from big gains in the countryside to losses in the densest blocks.

Watch it spread across the map

Every big metro core turns orange. By 2021, stores within about 7 km of the centre were down 26% in Charlotte, 30% in Raleigh, 33% in Durham, 21% in Greensboro and 15% in Winston-Salem. The ring 25–60 km out was up 18–21% around Charlotte, Greensboro and Winston-Salem, and roughly flat around the Triangle.

It happened inside the same chains

Maybe cities just have different stores? No. The same chain, with the same prices and the same shelves, lost ground in cities and gained it in the countryside.

Every kind of grocery store moved the same way

Two datasets, one answer

Phones count visits; cards count money. They come from different companies and different people. Store by store, they line up.

It never came back

After Advan's 2023 method change we restart the clock at 2023 = 100. City stores keep losing a little ground every year; rural stores keep gaining. Card spending, with no break at all, shows the city share still falling in 2024 and 2025.

Why? What we could check

We can see where people shopped, not why. One popular theory is that city stores lost commuters and office workers. The data does not show that clearly: weekday share barely moved, and city stores' visitors came from about as far away as before.

That leaves the explanations this data can't separate: people moving out to the suburbs and small towns, working from home and shopping close to home, and new stores opening where the growth is. For food access, the lesson is the same either way: the map of where North Carolina buys groceries changed in 2020 and has kept changing since.

Method and caveats

Stores. 4,452 grocery-type stores from the NC POI list, of which those matched to Advan foot traffic (98% of chains, 37% of independents) form the panels. Balanced panels keep only stores seen in 80%+ of weeks in each Advan era, so openings and closures don't drive the trend.

Place types. Each store takes the density of its nearest census block group: city 3,000+ people per square mile of land, suburban 500–3,000, rural under 500.

Fair comparison. A store's index is its visits divided by the average visits per store in the same Advan category across North Carolina that week. Advan changed its method in January 2023, so 2017–2022 and 2023–2026 are indexed separately and never compared.

Cards. SafeGraph Spend, monthly, for 1,834 of the same stores matched by placekey; the balanced card panel is 1,530 stores. It is an independent panel of card transactions, so agreement with the phone data is real cross-validation.

Limits. Phone and card panels are samples, not counts. Independent and ethnic grocers are under-represented. The pre-2020 drift means not all of the change is COVID. Data: Advan Weekly Patterns and SafeGraph Spend via Dewey (deweydata.io); ACS 2019–2023 5-year; Census TIGER 2023.
